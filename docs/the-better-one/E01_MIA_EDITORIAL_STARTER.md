## B. Audience value and the actual story engine

**Viewer's familiar false inference:** Full tables and busy staff = profitable business.

**Better understanding we want earned by the ending:** Being busy measures activity, not economic health. At full capacity, the business may lack enough *contribution after variable costs* per scarce unit of time/space to cover fixed costs and quality commitments. More demand can worsen workload without making the business viable.

**Dramatic human problem:** Mia is proud of her packed, beloved restaurant. But her closing ledger contradicts the applause. She has to choose which familiar plan to attempt and what to risk (staff, regulars, quality, time, identity of the restaurant). She must retain her own agency; the host investigates with her, not announce miracle fixes.

**Attractive first attempt (test, don't assume):** "Fill even more seats, offer discounts." Because the kitchen/time/seat bottleneck may already be saturated, lowering the net contribution per meal can enlarge the crowd and reduce profit. If more volume actually fits, model the capacity and incremental variable costs rather than narrating failure by decree.

**Master discovery candidate:** The scarce resource is not demand but profitable output through the bottleneck. The better move might be removing unprofitable menu complexity, reducing avoidable waste, redesigning preparation flow, or adding high-contribution items that require little scarce capacity. Each move has real costs, customer and staff effects and limited applicability. User must decide exact winning move after the model and story proof.

**Character ending:** an **earned conditional result**, not guaranteed rescue. It may be relief, qualified hope or an honest limit. Return to Mia and the actual human consequences, not a final chart.

## D. Demonstrative hypothetical unit model — from the Bible, NOT a business forecast

\`meals=180/day\`, \`price=$12\`, \`variable_cost=$9\`, \`other_daily_cost=$600\`.
Baseline contribution after variable cost: \`180*(12-9)=$540\`. Net after other daily cost = **−$60/day**.
Lower price to $10 without increasing already capped volume: \`180*(10-9)-600=−$420/day\`.
Hypothetical cost improvement to $8.40/meal and 40 incremental drinks with $3 contribution, **assuming no added overhead, labor/capacity or quality impact**:
\`180*(12-8.4)+40*3-600=+$168/day\`.

**Safety:** Real restaurant food and labor costs include variable and fixed portions; choose variable-cost definitions carefully to avoid double-counting. The example assumes the extra drinks have no additional bottleneck cost and customer behavior is unchanged—neither is a verified fact. Host must stress-test those assumptions, not call this proof of actual rescue. The emotional ending must be coherent with assumptions.
